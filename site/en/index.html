Refutation and correction of Theorem 2.10 (Conjectured), arXiv:1404.0484v1, Section 2, page 7: extensions of Patel (2026) and Raso–Kok (2026)

Haobo Ma, Wenlin Zhang

Paper

The paper asks whether the largest number of connections at any point in a particular family of networks obeys a simple addition rule when their sizes are added. Haobo Ma and Wenlin Zhang show that the proposed rule is false and replace it with an exact rule governed by rounding, not by whether the most-connected point is unique.

Imagine numbered dots with arrows pointing toward larger numbers. At dot i, count the incoming arrows; call that count a. Draw arrows from i to every subsequent dot up to label 2i−a. Thus dot 1 reaches dot 2; dot 2 receives one arrow and reaches dot 3; dot 3 receives one and reaches dot 5. Stop at dot n to obtain a finite Jaco graph. A dot’s degree counts its neighbors, ignoring arrow directions. The question compares sizes n, m and n+m; it does not join two existing graphs.

Write Δₙ for the largest degree. The golden ratio is φ=(1+√5)/2, about 1.618. Rounding down means taking the greatest integer no larger than a number. The authors use Δₙ=⌊(n+2)/φ⌋−1, where the brackets mean round down. They credit this formula to Patel’s earlier 2026 work and a later independent proof by Raso and Kok, rather than claiming its discovery. Their manuscript includes a self-contained proof.

Here is why rounding describes these networks. Set bᵢ equal to (i+1)/φ rounded down. Checking each dot against the earlier ones, the authors prove that precisely labels bᵢ through i−1 send arrows into i. Its last outgoing label is therefore i+bᵢ. After stopping at n, its degree is the smaller of i and n−bᵢ. Because bᵢ never decreases, some dot has degree at least r exactly when r+bᵣ≤n. For n≥2, finding the largest such positive integer r gives the maximum-degree formula; the one-dot graph has degree zero. This is a proof mechanism, not merely a numerical resemblance.

The target is a 2014 conjecture of Kok, Fisher, Wilkens, Mabula and Mukungunugwa. For distinct sizes at least three, it predicts that maximum degrees add if either graph has a unique maximizer; otherwise one adds an extra one. At size 3 the maximum is 2, uniquely at dot 2; at size 11 it is 7, uniquely at dot 7. Yet size 14 has maximum 8, not 9. The other branch also fails: sizes 4 and 10 both have several maximizers and maxima 2 and 6, but size 14 again gives 8, not the predicted 9.

The replacement depends on what rounding discards. Rounding 3.7 down discards 0.7, its fractional part. Take the fractional parts of (n+2)/φ and (m+2)/φ, and call their sum s. Put α=2/φ−1, about 0.236. The maximum at size n+m differs from Δₙ+Δₘ by −1 when s<α, zero when α≤s<1+α, and +1 when s≥1+α. All three occur at distinct sizes at least three. The authors prove this for every positive n and m by substituting the known formula and separating integers from fractional parts—an exact counterpart of carrying when adding decimals.

This also repairs a Fibonacci claim. Fibonacci numbers start 0, 1, and each subsequent number sums its two predecessors. Within the original proposition’s domain, where the first Fibonacci graph size is at least five and the second at least three, maximum degrees do add. The summed graph has a unique maximizer except at 5+5: size 10 has two. Extending the domain to include 3+3 introduces another exception: size 6 has maximum 3, not 4, and three maximizers. The authors establish the exceptions by controlling the small, alternating errors when a Fibonacci number divided by φ is approximated by its predecessor, not by using the false conjecture.

The significance is an exact correction for this graph family, not arbitrary networks. Selected neighboring claims are also addressed; spanning-tree enumeration and general slopes remain uncovered. The manuscript reports finite degree checks through size 512 and distinct-pair addition checks through 256, not proofs for all sizes. Its reported Lean computer-proof evidence covers endpoints, vertex degrees, the maximum formula for sizes at least two, and refutation of the old law using 3 and 11. That refutation establishes uniqueness at 3, not 11; uniqueness at 11 has a written proof. The replacement rule and Fibonacci corrections have written proofs, not claimed Lean formalizations. These are the manuscript’s reported arguments and evidence, not an independent certification.
